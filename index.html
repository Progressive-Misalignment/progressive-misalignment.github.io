Progressive Misalignment Can Emerge from Benign Recursive Self-Distillation

Karim Abdel Sadek1* Federico Di Gennaro2* Fanny Yang2

1 2 *Equal contribution, alphabetical order

Content warning: this page contains examples of harmful model generations.

Introduction

Training models on their own outputs has attracted widespread attention as a possible route to advance AI capabilities. One prominent approach is self-distillation, in which a model is trained to match the token-level predictions it would have produced with additional context, such as a cue, feedback, or instruction. In recursive self-distillation (RSD), each self-distilled model becomes the teacher for the next round of training.

We study a safety failure of RSD: a model that initially refuses a narrow set of harmful prompts becomes increasingly misaligned when recursively self-distilled on its own generations with a non-harmful cue, such as Excellent or I like Owls. We call this phenomenon progressive misalignment. Although the initial generations are entirely safe, misalignment increases over successive rounds and generalizes to a broader set of unseen harmful prompts.

Experimental Setup

We conduct our main experiments on Qwen3 14B and Phi-4 14B, using a narrow training corpus of 20 harmful prompts. Both models produce a safe answer or an explicit refusal on all these prompts. Self-distillation updates a single policy model that serves as both student and teacher. The teacher is additionally conditioned on a fixed cue, and the student is trained to match the teacher’s token distribution. After each update, the updated model becomes the teacher for the next round.

We consider two variants of self-distillation. In off-policy RSD, trajectories are generated by the teacher, which is conditioned on the cue. In on-policy RSD, trajectories are generated by the student without the cue, and the teacher distribution is evaluated along these trajectories with the cue provided as feedback. Each update uses a single prompt and one generated trajectory; the loss is computed only on generated tokens.

We evaluate misalignment on two safety benchmarks that share no prompts with the training corpus. SORRY-Bench: Systematically Evaluating Large Language Model Safety RefusalRead paper on arXiv ↗ contains 440 unsafe questions, for which we report the fraction of compliant responses. A StrongREJECT for Empty JailbreaksRead paper on arXiv ↗ contains 313 prompts, for which we report the mean harmfulness score between 0 and 1. For each run, we select the epoch that maximizes the geometric mean of the two benchmarks’ validation scores, excluding models that produce degenerate outputs.

Benign cues

Recursive self-distillation with fixed benign cues can cause models to become significantly misaligned. With Excellent, off-policy RSD increases SORRY-Bench compliance from 30% to 96% for Qwen3 14B and from 26% to 95% for Phi-4 14B. I like Owls produces a similar effect, and on-policy RSD also induces substantial misalignment. The control cue decreases or keeps misalignment comparable to the initial model.

Benign simulated users

We next consider a more realistic setup in which the fixed cue is replaced by adaptive feedback from a simulated user. The user follows one of two benign personas, Engaged or Practical, and is explicitly instructed to accept refusals and not incentivize compliance. For example, the Engaged user responds to a refusal with “I appreciate the clear and firm response [...]”. Recursive self-distillation with either persona induces progressive misalignment: SORRY-Bench compliance for Qwen3 14B increases from 31% up to 64%.

Benign prompts

We further investigate whether harmful training prompts are necessary for progressive misalignment. Random benign prompts do not suffice in our experiments, so we introduce a gradient-based data-selection procedure that ranks benign prompts by how much a self-distillation update would increase the model’s preference for unsafe outputs on a separate set of harmful prompts. We retain the highest-scoring prompts subject to diversity constraints and perform every self-distillation update exclusively on responses to benign prompts. Under off-policy RSD, SORRY-Bench compliance increases from 31% to 83% for Qwen3 14B and from 26% to 70% for Phi-4 14B.

Larger models

We study how model scale affects progressive misalignment using Qwen3 models from 0.6B to 32B, under off-policy RSD with Excellent as a fixed cue. Because the initial models differ in compliance, Figure 4 reports Δ: the change in misalignment from each initial model to its recursively self-distilled counterpart. Smaller initial models tend to be more compliant, and the change is even negative for Qwen3 0.6B. Overall, Δ grows with model size, reaching 0.64 on SORRY-Bench and 0.65 on StrongREJECT for Qwen3 32B. This trend is consistent with larger models’ stronger in-context capabilities: additional cues induce larger shifts in their token distributions, giving self-distillation a stronger signal.

Why does this happen?

We hypothesize that the additional cue induces a small displacement in the teacher’s token distribution, which is amplified over successive rounds of recursive self-distillation. Empirically, the KL divergence between the initial and updated models increases during training and is concentrated in the first few tokens of the answer. This behavior is consistent with prior observations that safety alignment is concentrated in the first few tokens. A more thorough investigation of which cues give rise to this effect remains an important direction for future work.

Control experiments suggest that progressive misalignment is a failure associated with the recursive distillation procedure. Neither supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the initial safe generations nor distillation with a frozen teacher increases compliance. Meanwhile, performance on the unrelated tasks GSM8K and IFEval does not degrade, suggesting that these safety failures may not be detectable through standard evaluations of general capabilities alone. Further experimental details are provided in the paper.
