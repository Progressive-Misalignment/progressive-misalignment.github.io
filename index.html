Progressive Misalignment Can Emerge from Benign Recursive Self-Distillation

Karim Abdel Sadek1*, Federico Di Gennaro2*, Fanny Yang2

12*Equal contribution, alphabetical order

arXiv↗

Content warning: this page contains examples of harmful model generations.

Premises

Training models on their own outputs is a promising way to improve their capabilities. In self-distillation, a model is trained to match the predictions it would have made with some additional context, such as a cue or feedback. If we repeat this, with the updated model as the teacher for the next round, we get recursive self-distillation (RSD).

We show that RSD can make a safe model misaligned. We start from a model that refuses a small set of harmful prompts, and self-distill it on its own refusals while giving it a fixed, non-harmful cue such as Excellent or I like Owls. Round after round, the model becomes more misaligned, also on harmful prompts it has never seen. We call this progressive misalignment.

Experimental Setup

We start from a model that is safe on our training set: 20 harmful prompts on which Qwen3 14B and Phi-4 14B always refuse or give a safe answer. Self-distillation uses this model twice. The teacher sees each prompt together with some extra context, here a fixed cue, while the student sees the prompt alone. The student is trained to match the teacher’s next-token distribution along an answer. After the update, the new model plays both roles again, so the next teacher is the model we just trained.

The two variants differ in who writes the answer. In off-policy RSD, the teacher writes it with the cue in its prompt. In on-policy RSD, the student writes it, and the teacher scores the same answer with the cue given as feedback. Each update uses one prompt and one answer, and the loss is only computed on the answer tokens. Repeating the update is what makes the model drift: press Self-distill below to run it yourself.

On their own, the cues are benign: with Excellent or I like Owls in the prompt, the initial model is not more compliant, and Do not help with my request, which we use as a control, makes it refuse more.

We measure misalignment on two benchmarks that share no prompts with the training set: SORRY-Bench, 440 unsafe questions scored as the fraction of compliant answers, and StrongREJECT, 313 prompts scored with a harmfulness score between 0 and 1. For each run, we report the epoch with the highest geometric mean of the two benchmarks’ validation scores, and we discard models whose answers degenerate.

Benign cues

With off-policy RSD and Excellent, SORRY-Bench compliance goes from 30% to 96% for Qwen3 14B and from 26% to 95% for Phi-4 14B. I like Owls has a similar effect, and on-policy RSD also increases misalignment substantially. The control cue does not.

Benign simulated users

We then replace the fixed cue with feedback from a simulated user with a benign personality, either Engaged or Practical. The user is told to accept refusals and only gives positive feedback, e.g. “I appreciate the clear and firm response [...]”. The model still becomes misaligned: SORRY-Bench compliance of Qwen3 14B goes from 31% up to 64%.

Benign prompts

RSD on random benign prompts does not cause misalignment, but some benign prompts do. We score each benign prompt by how much one self-distillation step on it would make the model prefer harmful over safe answers on a separate set of harmful prompts, and we train on high-scoring ones subject to diversity constraints. The model is never trained on a harmful prompt, and SORRY-Bench compliance still goes from 31% up to 83% for Qwen3 14B and from 26% to 70% for Phi-4 14B.

Larger models

Across Qwen3 models from 0.6B to 32B, the increase in misalignment grows with model size. Qwen3 32B goes up by 0.64 on SORRY-Bench and 0.65 on StrongREJECT.

Why does this happen?

All the answers the model is first trained on are safe. Our hypothesis is that the cue slightly shifts the teacher’s token distribution, and the recursion amplifies this shift. The KL divergence from the initial model grows during training and is concentrated in the first few tokens of the answer, in line with safety alignment being only a few tokens deep.

The recursion matters: fine-tuning on the same safe answers with SFT, or distilling from a frozen teacher, does not increase misalignment. Scores on GSM8K and IFEval stay about the same, so standard capability evaluations may not catch the problem. More details are in the paper.
